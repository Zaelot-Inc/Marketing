## The old way

Here's how a migration has worked for the last 25 years:

1. A project owner lists every page in a spreadsheet.
2. Content editors or junior developers copy each page into the new CMS, fix the links, and re-upload the images.
3. Developers rebuild every page template by hand so the new site can show the content.
4. QA testers check a sample of the work, because checking every page would take too long.

Add it up and a major migration runs 5,000+ hours. Four people for eight months. That's your $500K quote.

The bigger the site, the bigger the bill. Manual processes almost guarantee there are bugs and pages that need to be fixed post-launch. Manual migration was a messy, stressful process.

## It used to be just tech debt

AI made that debt a lot more expensive. Now you pay for it in three places:

**Your visibility.** LLMs thrive on well-structured content: clear content types, FAQs, and pages built for GEO. Legacy CMSs and old front ends give them very little to read. So when your customers ask ChatGPT, it answers with someone else's content.

**Your speed.** Teams on modern stacks use AI to ship FAQs, comparison pages, and localized content in days. When every page needs an engineer, you can't keep up. Your team's hours go into tickets and copy and paste instead.

**Your AI tools.** AI writing, translation, and personalization tools, including Contentful AI Actions, need structured content they can reach through APIs. A legacy CMS keeps your content out of their reach.

**The migration got cheaper. Waiting didn't.**

## Migrations aren't done page by page anymore

AI agents now do the page-by-page work. People make the decisions. Here's what that looks like:

1. **Every page gets looked at first.** An agent goes through the whole site and sorts every page by type. When the structure was never written down, it reads the pages and the code together. Pages that don't fit get flagged with a reason. If content lives in several CMSs, each extra one is a small added step, not a new project.

2. **The content model gets written down, and people sign off.** Your team and the builders agree on the content model: the content types every page will be built from. Agents suggest where each piece of old content should go. People approve or change each suggestion. Nothing gets deleted without a person signing off.

3. **The new site gets built from the plan.** Agents build each template and its building blocks from the same content model, so the content and the site fit together from day one. Builders spend their time reviewing code instead of writing every template by hand.

4. **Every page gets checked, not a sample.** Machines compare the old site to the new one: every field, link, image, and redirect, in every language, plus how each page looks. Anything that doesn't match goes to a person before launch day.

5. **Launch gets rehearsed until it's boring.** Because the process runs automatically, the team can do full practice runs again and again. On launch day, the old site stays ready as a backup.

This works on the two migrations we see most often: several CMSs behind one website that need to become one, and a vibe-coded or no-code site (Builder, Framer, Lovable, Bolt, v0) that a growing team has outgrown.
