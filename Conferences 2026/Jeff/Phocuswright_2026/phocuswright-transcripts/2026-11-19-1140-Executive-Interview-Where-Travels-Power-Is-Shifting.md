## Summary

The conversation opened with Hopper's founder clarifying recent press coverage: he had not left the company and Hopper did not sell itself to Capital One, but rather licensed software and helped Capital One build its own travel team, with Capital One remaining a customer. He also confirmed that Dakota, who co-founded the B2B (HTS) business and worked there for 13 years, left to start his own venture after discussing the move for some time, and that the split was amicable.

On the broader trend of banks building in-house travel capabilities, it was explained that the three largest US banks sell roughly $30 billion in travel annually, growing 22% a year versus 4-6% for the wider category, a dynamic rooted in Amex's legacy loyalty-banking-travel expertise now spreading across Chase, Capital One, and internationally. Hopper's HTS network now connects to about 400 million credit card holders, moves nearly 2 million loyalty points a day, and operates in 23 countries, powering major banks in Australia and Japan, with the business expected to double next year; Capital One taught Hopper credit cards and loyalty while Hopper taught them travel.

On the consumer-facing Hopper app, it was described as far from abandoned: nearly 13 years old, still used by millions, maintaining steady app usage while its website grows 25% year over year, and still skewing toward young users who develop long-term brand loyalty. The app was credited as essential groundwork for building HTS, since operating at scale across many regulatory environments gave Hopper the operating history and fintech infrastructure needed to serve banking partners, even though it will never match Booking.com in scale.

Asked whether banks like Capital One or Chase could become the next big OTA, the response pivoted to a more provocative thesis: it is unclear whether marketplaces of any kind survive the shift to agentic AI. Citing Meta's push of a fully autonomous agent to billions of users, AI was framed as the first self-improving, exponentially advancing technology, unlike prior innovations such as the telephone, implying aggregators that merely compare and display data may become obsolete as agents do the shopping and booking directly. This logic extended to Google itself, said to be deliberately cannibalizing its own highly profitable search business by shifting toward generative answers despite a $600 billion market-cap hit, suggesting the wider industry will follow suit rapidly once 'fast' change kicks in, with civilization unrecognizable within a decade; meanwhile payment rails, fintech, protection products, and inventory were seen as more likely to endure than digital comparison surfaces, including the Hopper app itself, and the scale of recent AI-related IPOs was presented as evidence of how large and fast this shift already is.

On practical implications, it was suggested OTAs should focus on distributing their core inventory, payments, and commerce capabilities outward to whatever agentic platforms emerge, rather than defending the comparison layer, with Booking and Expedia cited as already moving this direction. Blocking AI agents, as in disputes like United and Chase restricting bot access, was compared to suing customers over Napster and seen as untenable, especially as over half of internet traffic is now non-human. Looking ahead, areas seen as resistant to AI disruption included human trust, meaningful relationships, and functions requiring accountability, even as AI is poised to take over high-error-rate domains like medicine; the session closed by endorsing the view that this is an unprecedented moment to build new companies, with more generational businesses expected to emerge in the coming years.

## Key Takeaways

### 🤖 AI agents could disintermediate travel marketplaces

Lalonde believes autonomous agents may handle travel planning, shopping and booking directly, weakening OTAs and other aggregators. He sees payment rails, fintech, protection products and supplier inventory as more durable than consumer-facing digital surfaces and comparison databases.

Quotes:

> In a world where these agents are learning how to do everything for us, it is unclear that you need an aggregator in the middle.

### 📱 Hopper’s consumer business remains active and profitable

Hopper has not abandoned its consumer business: millions still use the app, its website is growing 25% year over year, and the B2C operation remains profitable. The consumer product also serves as a testing and regulatory-learning ground for HTS.

Quotes:

> I don't think we could have built HTS without having—you know, like, literally tens of millions of users going through our Hopper stack. It's profitable.

### 🏦 Bank-led travel is rapidly expanding through Hopper’s loyalty network

Lalonde says bank-led travel, commerce and loyalty is growing about 22% annually—far faster than the broader travel market—and that Hopper’s B2B platform now operates in 23 countries, connects to about 400 million cardholders and moves nearly 2 million loyalty points per day.

Quotes:

> That segment, the bank-led travel, commerce, and loyalty, is growing 22% a year, where the general category is growing about 4 to 6%... Today we're connected to about 400 million credit card holders. We move almost 2 million points a day.

### 🚀 AI is creating a rare window for new companies

Lalonde believes the pace of AI change will create an unusually favorable period for startups and corporate innovators. He argues that the next five years could produce generational companies and that builders should act during this transition rather than wait for the market to stabilize.

Quotes:

> If you're a startup or you're building or you're innovating in your company, statistically, you will not have an opportunity like the next 5 years in your entire lifetime. This is when generational companies will be built.

### 🤝 Human trust and relationships may remain durable

As AI takes over more operational and professional tasks, Lalonde sees meaningful personal and business relationships as one area likely to retain value. He is less certain that creativity will remain exclusively human, but considers relationship-building a positive long-term investment.

Quotes:

> Where we are investing are human time, is in relationship building. And I will at least say, on a positive note, if you are building meaningful relationships with other people, personally or professionally, I think there's a future in that.

### 🔌 Make core travel commerce available to future channels

As distribution changes, Lalonde says travel companies should make their core value—inventory, payments and commerce—available to whatever interfaces or agents emerge, rather than depend on a particular consumer-facing marketplace.

Quotes:

> The one thing I know is the future is taking the core value of your product, the commerce, the payment, the inventory, and making it available for whatever's coming next.

### 📈 Self-improving AI could accelerate travel disruption

Lalonde distinguishes AI from earlier technologies because it improves as it operates, creating nonlinear change that could rapidly reshape travel distribution and other knowledge-based work.

Quotes:

> This is the first time as a species that we have built a technology that is learning... This is the first self-improving innovation that we have created as a species. And it's nonlinear. It's getting better at an exponential rate.

### 🧠 Travel software is shifting from databases to intelligence

Lalonde argues that AI is reducing the strategic importance of manually structured databases, APIs and fixed user interfaces. Companies increasingly need to convert disparate data into intelligence that agents can use.

Quotes:

> We are now all in the business of converting data into intelligence.

### 🔎 Generative AI is forcing Google to cannibalize search

Lalonde says generative answers threaten the blue-link search model that has captured most travel advertising profit. Because AI solves problems within the interface rather than sending users to other sites, Google may be sacrificing near-term search monetization to adapt to the new model.

Quotes:

> AI is not about sending the eyeballs of the user to another surface. It's about solving the problem in the place. And nobody knows how to monetize that, including Google.

### 🚧 Blocking customer agents is unlikely to work

Lalonde argues that travel companies cannot treat autonomous agents as hostile bots if those agents are acting on behalf of their customers. He compares blocking them with earlier attempts to fight changing distribution technologies and says the industry must instead determine how to serve agent-mediated demand securely.

Quotes:

> Blocking agents that are used by a billion people is like when Sony Music sued a housewife in Kentucky for using Napster. If you sue your customers, they're going to go buy your thing somewhere else.
