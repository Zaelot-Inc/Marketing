## Summary

The session opened with an executive interview reflecting on the pitches just seen on stage, framed around how to build the next generation of travel. The guest, who co-founded and led Concur before its sale to SAP and now works with Madrona as a travel investor while running another startup, said he was having the time of his life riding the AI wave, praising the founders who had pitched for their courage, but stressed that what matters most is what founders take from the panelists' questions rather than their own answers. What keeps him up at night is the industry's tendency to lose sight of who the real customer is: the individual travel buyer and, equally important but historically underserved, the travel seller, with everyone else occupying a shrinking middle layer that needs to be clear about the value it adds.

On the day's pitches, the feedback was that too many startups are trying to solve too many problems at once rather than nailing one; the advice was to pick a single problem and own it. Beyond front-end innovation, the infrastructure layer of travel is also being rebuilt, and without that rebuild new ventures risk hitting a wall imposed by legacy channel and business models. Three of the companies on stage were singled out as ones worth backing, provided they tighten their focus.

A major thread was the current wave of agentic travel agents, which was described as repeating the industry's historic obsession with the 5% of the journey spent buying rather than the 95% spent traveling, where most dissatisfaction and cost actually occur. United's recent decision to shut off agentic agents was read as evidence that bombarding supplier sites with purchase requests only pushes cost onto human service agents without driving incremental revenue, since the booking already belongs to the supplier. The conclusion was that agentic agents need to become trusted gateways into content and purchasing and deliver value beyond transactions, or they will not survive. The same conference-wide fascination with AI prompted a separate question about what actually distinguishes an AI-native company from one that has simply added AI to its pitch deck; the answer centered on reimagining workflows without being anchored to legacy processes, and on using real user data and behavior to improve the experience, rather than just layering AI onto old systems. Legacy technology was framed as worth understanding but not worth being bound by, drawing a direct parallel to how expense reporting itself was reinvented rather than built on top of what already existed.

On corporate travel and fintech convergence, the discussion covered fintechs like Ramp and Brex (and Capital One's acquisition of Brex) reinventing the expense economics that once underpinned Concur, subsidizing software costs in exchange for card transaction volume, a shift praised as a lesson that no company can rest on its model. The content and distribution model is also moving away from reliance on GDS infrastructure, which is breaking automated, predictive servicing capabilities that need to be rebuilt. Knowing the traveler was framed as requiring a much richer data graph than travel history alone, built in a way that earns durable trust rather than simply monetizing data, since trust is what retains customers long-term.

Looking at the broader vision behind the guest's current venture, the goal remains delivering a 'perfect trip' infrastructure, originally conceived for corporate travel but now extended toward leisure, enabled by cloud-native, integrated data architectures that didn't exist a decade ago but still largely don't exist industry-wide today. The consumer opportunity was judged larger than corporate, with the two experiences converging except for duty of care and policy compliance, which remain corporate travel's distinguishing requirements. The session closed with advice to founders to hold a broad vision and architecture while concentrating immediate execution on solving one problem so well that a customer cannot live without it.

## Key Takeaways

### 🎯 Solve one specific customer problem

Travel startups should define exactly whom they serve and focus tightly on one specific problem rather than pursuing an overly broad scope.

Quotes:

> “This is too much of what I saw today: we're trying to solve too many problems, too broad of a scope. Pick one problem, nail that problem.”

### 🤝 Treat travel sellers as a second customer

Travel sellers are another important customer group, and the industry has not served them well enough.

Quotes:

> “There is a second customer and in my view that second customer has not been well served. That's the travel seller.”

### 🧠 Use a trusted, richer data graph of the traveler

Understanding a traveler requires more than travel history or TMC records. Platforms should combine broader information about the individual to understand decisions across different personas, then use that data to improve the experience and earn long-term trust rather than primarily drive revenue.

Quotes:

> “I want to know more about your life and then really build a data graph on that individual in a way that understands the decisions they make in the different personas in which they operate… If we use that information to go drive a better experience for the customer that we're trying to serve, that trust is everything.”

### 💳 Expect card economics to reshape corporate travel software

Card companies can use travel and expense software to drive more transactions, potentially making software heavily subsidized and overturning the traditional corporate-travel economics associated with standalone platforms.

Quotes:

> “The equation that Concur operated with is no longer valid… it's going to be reinvented in an economic model where the software costs are massively subsidized.”

### 🧳 Put the traveler at the center

Singh argues that the individual consuming travel services—not only the corporate buyer or organizational intermediary—should be recognized as the primary customer.

Quotes:

> “For me in the travel space I think we should identify that the travel buyer, the individual, the person who's actually consuming these travel services, they're the customer.”

### 🛠️ Build an open infrastructure for the perfect trip

Travel innovation will stall without modern infrastructure: an integrated, open data model and systems that communicate across suppliers, channels and service operations. As content moves beyond the GDS, the industry must also reinvent how suppliers differentiate and merchandise content and how travel is serviced digitally, proactively and predictively.

Quotes:

> “The concept of the perfect trip shouldn't exist just for the corporate environment… it requires… an entirely integrated data model, an integrated set of systems that actually talk to one another in a completely open model.”

### 🔄 Make AI-native products rethink the workflow

An AI-native company should not simply add AI to an existing product. It should reimagine the underlying process by questioning legacy workflows and using user data to make the experience fundamentally better.

Quotes:

> “There's also a reimagining of that particular process. Like, how can a particular process be delivered in a world where you don't have to think about the legacy workflows?”

### 🌍 Bring consumer delight into corporate travel

Consumer travel is the larger opportunity, and corporate and leisure experiences are converging. The main distinctions that remain for corporate travel are duty of care, policy, compliance and negotiated requirements—not the basic expectation of a seamless, enjoyable experience.

Quotes:

> “The experiences between corporate and consumer are getting closer and closer together… the difference between corporate and consumer is going to be duty of care, understanding corporate policy and compliance… but the rest of the experience should be just as delightful as what you can get in a consumer experience.”

### 🧑‍💼 Build specialized agents around trusted context

The market will include many category-specific agents alongside broad assistants. Travel agents should use deep, trusted knowledge of a traveler’s preferences and circumstances—like an exceptional executive assistant—to deliver specialized value at far lower cost.

Quotes:

> “There'll be lots of them… you'll have specialized agents… pick a category, be exceptional in that, but you're a subcategory to the broader-based agents.”

### 🧳 Focus innovation on the trip, not the booking

Most travel innovation targets the small purchase window, even though the larger source of customer frustration occurs throughout the journey—from leaving home until returning.

Quotes:

> “The vast majority of innovation is on how you sell… Very little innovation is on how do you actually service that. Most of the problems that you have in a trip are not in the 5% of the time to actually buy something; it's in the 95% of the time from the moment you leave your front door through the trip and all the way back.”
