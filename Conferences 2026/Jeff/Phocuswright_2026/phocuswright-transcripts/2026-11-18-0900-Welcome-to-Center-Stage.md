## Summary

The session opened with event housekeeping and recognitions: organizers reaffirmed the carbon-offset partnership with Sustainable Travel International, reintroduced the GetPika photo tool, and welcomed nearly 500 first-time attendees alongside many veterans. The Philip C. Wolfe Scholarship recipient, Lola C. of Tripsetta, was recognized, and a new feature offering live transcripts and summaries of Center Stage sessions in six languages was announced. The newly published Hot 25 Travel Startups list for 2027, sponsored by Amex GBT, was highlighted, noting that one honoree, Odessia, had already been acquired by OpenAI the day after the list's release. The 25th anniversary of parent company Northstar Travel Group was marked, and three upcoming dates were flagged: the Travel Forward outlook publication on December 9th, the Travel Marketing AI Summit in New York on March 1st, and Phocuswright Europe's move to London in late May.

The program's framing theme—pressures, power, pivots—was introduced by pointing to a new generation of AI agents that have rapidly moved from concept to product and are reshaping the interface between travelers and travel companies. This was placed alongside other forces squeezing the industry: shifting consumer expectations, margin pressure, new competitors, regulatory scrutiny, and the difficulty of modernizing legacy systems while still running them, with Friday's FAA ground stop cited as a vivid example. These pressures were framed as shifting power over who controls discovery, who owns the traveler relationship, who accesses demand, and who holds leverage in the value chain—forcing companies into pivots about what to build, defend, or abandon.

Despite these pressures, Phocuswright's newly published Global Travel Market Report shows resilient demand, projecting global gross travel bookings will exceed $1.7 trillion by year-end and reach $1.9 trillion by 2029, with North America remaining the largest but slowest-growing market (3.5% through 2028) while Europe, Asia Pacific, and the Middle East grow near 5% annually and Latin America leads at nearly 10% from a smaller base. Yet strong demand coexists with rising traveler anxiety: research conducted for Protect Group found half of U.S. international travelers report feeling more anxious booking trips than two to three years ago, driven by worries over cost, disruption, and recourse when things go wrong. Internally, companies face their own pressure, with nearly half of surveyed travel firms citing lack of expertise or time—rather than cost—as the biggest barrier to implementing AI, a point underscored by an informal room poll on whether companies feel they are moving fast enough.

Traveler trust in AI is rising quickly: 41% of U.S. travelers trust AI results on travel sites, roughly matching trust in human reviews, with Gen Z and millennials even higher at about 56%. Among those familiar with AI, 43% say they would book inside a platform like ChatGPT or Google's AI mode, though trust drops sharply at checkout, with only 35% willing to pay inside an AI platform. This pattern echoed a nearly 30-year-old Phocuswright study of 'wired travelers' in the late 1990s, when the vast majority had never bought travel online and distrusted entering credit card details on websites, instead using the internet for research before booking through agents or airlines directly—concerns that faded as experience grew, eventually leading to about two-thirds of travel being booked online today. The parallel suggests AI may be in an early research-before-transaction phase similar to the web's early adoption curve.

A gap was identified between consumer readiness and industry readiness: while 43% of travelers say they'd book inside an AI platform, only 28% of surveyed companies are building or planning bookable inventory there, a divide described as potentially becoming a power gap rather than just a technology gap. Further data showed 45% of companies are scaling agentic AI but only 9% feel fully prepared, and startups are far more likely than established companies to have redesigned core workflows around AI (53% versus 18%). This set up the agenda ahead, including Steve Hafner's shift from building Kayak to building an AI assistant inside Booking Holdings, perspectives from Booking Holdings' Omri Morgenstern and Expedia Group's Alfonso Paredes on the growing power of B2B and behind-the-scenes sellers, and further Phocuswright Research on AI deployment and traveler use of AI alongside human advice. With nearly two-thirds of attendees at C-level or VP rank and representation from 36 countries, the session closed by framing the two days ahead as an opportunity to understand which pressures matter, where power is shifting, and which pivots are worth making.

## Key Takeaways

### 🔌 AI booking demand is outpacing industry readiness

Travelers familiar with AI are more ready to book inside AI platforms than travel companies are ready to make their inventory bookable there, creating a potential competitive and distribution gap.

Quotes:

> "43% say they'd book travel inside an AI platform, but only 28% of companies we surveyed are building or even planning to make their inventory bookable there."

### ⚙️ Agentic AI adoption is ahead of preparedness

Many travel companies are scaling agentic AI, but few consider themselves fully prepared, underscoring the need to build organizational capability alongside new tools.

Quotes:

> "45% of surveyed companies are scaling or have scaled agentic AI, yet just 9% say they are fully prepared."

### 📈 Travel demand remains resilient but regionally uneven

Global travel gross bookings are projected to exceed $1.7 trillion this year and reach $1.9 trillion by 2029, but growth varies substantially by region. Latin America is projected to grow fastest, while North America remains the largest and slowest-growing major market.

Quotes:

> Phocuswright projects global gross bookings will be more than $1.7 trillion by the end of the year and $1.9 trillion by the end of 2029. Latin America is projected to grow at nearly 10% annually, compared with 3.5% in North America.

### 🤝 B2B platforms are becoming trust infrastructure

The growing importance of B2B reflects a power shift in which companies increasingly enable trusted sellers behind the scenes, influencing how travel is distributed without always owning the consumer-facing relationship.

Quotes:

> "The growing importance of B2B and the companies increasingly power trust sellers behind the scenes."

### 🏗️ Modernization must confront legacy-system risk

Travel businesses must modernize while continuing to operate existing systems, creating disruption risks when new technology meets legacy infrastructure.

Quotes:

> "The industry is also dealing with ... the challenge of modernizing businesses while continuing to run them." The FAA ground stop was described as a dramatic example of what can happen when modernization meets legacy infrastructure.

### 🚀 Startups are redesigning around AI faster

Startups are more likely than established travel companies to redesign core workflows around AI, suggesting that organizational structure and legacy processes may affect the pace of adoption.

Quotes:

> "53% of startups strongly agree they've redesigned core workflows around AI, versus 18% of established companies."

### ⏳ AI adoption is constrained more by capacity than cost

For travel companies implementing AI, the leading obstacles are a lack of expertise or time; cost is a concern for a smaller share of respondents.

Quotes:

> When we asked travel companies about the biggest challenges in implementing AI, nearly half of respondents cited a lack of expertise or time. Cost was a concern for fewer than a third.

### 🔀 AI is shifting control across the travel value chain

As AI changes how travelers discover and purchase travel, companies must decide what to build, defend, or stop doing as control of discovery, traveler relationships, demand, and leverage moves.

Quotes:

> "Who controls discovery? Who owns the traveler relationship? Who gets access to demand? And who has leverage in the value chain?"

### 🤖 AI is becoming a trusted but incomplete travel interface

AI agents are moving from concept to product and becoming interfaces between travelers and travel companies. Travelers increasingly trust AI recommendations and are willing to book within AI platforms, but confidence falls when it comes to completing payment.

Quotes:

> "These tools are quickly becoming new interfaces between travelers and travel companies." Among U.S. travelers, 41% trust AI results on travel sites, and 43% of travelers familiar with AI say they would book inside an AI platform, but only 35% trust paying inside one.

### 😟 Travelers want trips but feel more exposed

Travel demand is strong, but travelers are more anxious about booking than they were a few years ago, especially regarding costs, disruptions, and what happens when something goes wrong.

Quotes:

> "Half of U.S. international travelers say they're more anxious when booking trips than they were 2 to 3 years ago."
