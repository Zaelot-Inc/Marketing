## Summary

The session opened by framing how AI assistants are changing travel search and increasingly enabling bookings, pushing boards to ask what companies are doing to be ready. Connecting to these channels via MCPs was described as the right first step, but the deeper, harder work comes after that connection is made: ensuring unit economic strategy, differentiation, and infrastructure are truly ready for AI channels, since flows for search, booking, and servicing will all change even as metasearch and direct channels continue to drive significant volume.

On search, AI assistants let travelers ask much more complex and detailed queries beyond simply the lowest price, including refund and change fees, branded fare options, and loyalty benefits. Delivering this richer information earlier in the search phase increases calls to suppliers, straining unit economics and look-to-book ratios, and agencies that fail to provide this detail risk becoming less attractive to both travelers and AI assistants. Traditional methods relying on live supplier calls or search-time caching struggle to deliver the needed coverage, accuracy, and detail, so the proposed alternative is prediction at search: combining airline policy intelligence with historical data to predict structured fare rules and branded fare pricing in advance, achieving higher coverage, strong accuracy, and lower cost than organic search-time lookups.

On booking, AI assistants threaten to disrupt the traditional OTA funnel model that relies on upsells and cross-sells, since a traveler could book a flight with one OTA via an AI assistant and then be referred elsewhere for hotel, car, or insurance. Until new monetization avenues emerge, the economics of the flight booking itself become far more critical. The suggested response is a deeper layer of profitability intelligence that makes forecasting more granular, helping set prices that convert while remaining profitable, alongside differentiating the offer beyond price—such as enabling a bundled fare with 96-hour cancel-for-any-reason coverage to help partners stand out.

On servicing, as AI makes search and booking faster and easier, servicing may become the main point where travelers actually interact with an agency's brand, making fast, frictionless resolution a key differentiator for loyalty and for being recommended by AI assistants. The core problem is that travel infrastructure remains broken: roughly 60% of servicing tasks still depend on costly manual work, with the hardest cases involving unstructured fare rules, virtual interlining, multiple passengers and forms of payment, and waivers across channels like NDC, LCC, BSP Link, and ACH/ADM disputes. The bottleneck is automated execution rather than conversation, and the described approach—developed over nearly a decade by going deep rather than wide, solving edge case after edge case with a zero-human-in-the-loop mindset and learning from real servicing data—aims to close that execution gap.

The session concluded that while the pace and scale of AI assistants reshaping travel remain uncertain, agencies need to start preparing now, and that the work done to support AI assistants—improving search technology, refining pricing, differentiating beyond price, and automating servicing—will strengthen flight strategy and infrastructure more broadly, making flights a stronger growth driver regardless of where a traveler's journey begins. The closing exchange reiterated that servicing automation must go beyond handling the simple, common cases to address the full range of complex scenarios.

## Key Takeaways

### 🔌 MCP connectivity is only the starting point

Connecting to AI channels through MCPs is described as an important first step, but agencies must also ensure their unit economics, differentiation, and infrastructure are ready for these channels.

Quotes:

> It's not only about connecting MCPs. It goes much deeper.

### 🏗️ Backend automation must resolve servicing edge cases

Wenrix describes a backend-focused approach to closing the servicing execution gap by tackling complex cases one by one and aiming for zero human intervention. Examples include unstructured fare rules, virtual interlining, multiple passengers or payment methods, and waivers across fragmented channels; around 60% of servicing work still depends heavily on costly manual effort.

Quotes:

> The bottleneck isn't conversation, it's automated execution... solving edge case after edge case with the mindset of zero human in the loop.

### ⚖️ Richer search data creates a unit-economic tradeoff

Surfacing detailed fare and policy information earlier in the search journey can require more supplier calls, increasing costs and putting pressure on unit economics and look-to-book ratios. Agencies must balance that burden against the competitive need to provide clearer information.

Quotes:

> Providing this information earlier in the search phase means more calls to the suppliers, creating an extra burden on unit economic and look-to-book ratios.

### ✨ Differentiated offers can compete beyond price

Agencies can stand out in AI-mediated booking by adding meaningful product value rather than competing only on fare price, such as a bundled fare with 96-hour cancel-for-any-reason protection.

Quotes:

> We enable our partners to offer a bundled fare that includes 96 hours cancel for any reason in order for them to really stand out.

### 🧠 Prediction at search can enrich fare intelligence

Wenrix says agencies can combine airline policy intelligence with historical data to predict structured fare rules and higher branded-fare prices during search. This is intended to provide broader coverage and high accuracy at lower cost than relying only on organic supplier searches or caching.

Quotes:

> Combining airline policy intelligence together with historical data, we predict already at search detailed and structured fare rules and prices of higher branded fares.

### 🔀 AI assistants may split the OTA cross-sell journey

A traveler could book a flight through one OTA and then be referred by the AI assistant to different suppliers for hotels, car rental, or insurance. This threatens the cross-sell assumptions behind traditional OTA funnel economics.

Quotes:

> A traveler may book a flight through an AI assistant with one OTA and then be referred to another supplier for the hotel car rental or insurance.

### 🛎️ Servicing may become the key brand interaction

As AI makes search and booking easier and faster, servicing could become the main stage where travelers directly experience an agency's brand. Fast, frictionless resolution can help agencies build loyalty and earn recommendations.

Quotes:

> As search and book become easier and faster with AI, servicing may be the only place your traveler really interacts with your brand.

### 🧭 AI preparation can strengthen the broader flight strategy

The work required for AI assistants can deliver benefits beyond those channels: better search technology, more refined pricing, stronger differentiation beyond price, and automated servicing can make flights a stronger growth driver wherever the traveler journey begins.

Quotes:

> The work that you will do for AI assistants will help you improve search technology, refine your pricing, stand out beyond price alone, and finally automate servicing.

### 🔎 AI search requires richer, clearer travel information

AI-assisted travelers ask broader questions than which itinerary is cheapest. Agencies increasingly need to surface refund and change fees, branded fare options, loyalty benefits, and other structured details early in search; failing to provide accurate, useful information can make them less attractive to travelers and AI assistants.

Quotes:

> Travelers now look for much more information when they search: refund and change fees, branded fare options, loyalty benefits, and more.

### 💰 Flight-level profitability becomes more important

If cross-sell and upsell opportunities weaken and replacement monetization has not yet emerged, the economics of the flight itself become more consequential. More granular profitability forecasting can help set offers that convert while remaining profitable.

Quotes:

> Until new monetization opportunities emerge the economics of the flight itself become much more important.

### 📊 Servicing data can improve automation

Wenrix says real servicing data— including airline policies and agents' actions—can help agencies understand how rules are applied and improve automated servicing, particularly across complex workflows.

Quotes:

> Our approach is to rely on servicing data, real servicing data, to understand better the airline policies and agents' actions.
