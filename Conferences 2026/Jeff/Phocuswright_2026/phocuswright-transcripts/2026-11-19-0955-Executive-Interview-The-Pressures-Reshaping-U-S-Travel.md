## Summary

The session opened with a discussion of the cruise sector's post-pandemic strength, framing the conversation around how the U.S. consumer's relationship with travel has fundamentally shifted. The cruise executive explained that prioritization of travel, long a cultural norm in Europe, has finally caught on with Americans since the pandemic, moving from a temporary 'revenge travel' phenomenon five years ago to a lasting change in how people choose to live their lives and allocate spending.

On resilience in the face of economic and shock-driven pressures, it was argued that consumers will cut other spending before giving up travel experiences, and that cruising's relative value versus other vacation options provides some insulation during downturns. Shock events such as groundings, geopolitical conflict, or policy disruptions tend to cause temporary pauses in booking as people normalize new information about their jobs, savings, and the future, but historically travelers have returned to booking en masse once they adjust, as seen even after a prolonged Middle East conflict. This resilience was echoed in broader industry data showing record quarters for hotels and record spring break, Memorial Day, and July 4th travel volumes, despite the mounting number of disruptions.

A significant portion of the conversation focused on how booking behavior is being reshaped by AI. While traditional travel agents will remain relevant for some customers in the medium term, and direct digital booking continues to grow, AI assistants and agentic platforms are seen as disrupting the entire booking landscape. Princess Cruises was cited as the first cruise brand to launch plugins for ChatGPT and Claude, allowing bookings to begin within those platforms, with a stated expectation that within single-digit years consumers may complete entire bookings through AI assistants rather than websites, fundamentally shifting power toward the consumer. This transition is already prompting a reallocation rather than simple increase of marketing and distribution spend, with channels like SEO becoming less valuable while AI-driven efficiencies are being applied across the P&L to boost both cost savings and revenue generation.

On costs, the company does not hedge fuel and instead focuses on efficiency, having cut fuel consumption per unit by about 26% since 2019 and absolute greenhouse gas emissions by 12% despite being larger. Pricing, it was stressed, is not cost-based but driven by what consumers are willing to pay, meaning input cost swings in fuel, food, and labor do not mechanically pass through to fares the way they might in other travel sectors like airlines. Even amid a sustained geopolitical conflict that many feared would cause broader economic disruption, the anticipated worst-case outcomes largely did not materialize, reinforcing a view that the industry adapts to supply chain and geopolitical volatility rather than planning around a stable baseline.

The discussion closed on man-made disruptions and policy, including recent government shutdowns, TSA line backups, air traffic controller shortages, and a steep decline in Canadian travel to the U.S., all described as self-inflicted drags on an otherwise resilient industry that have cost the company a few hundred million dollars in lost revenue and a point or two of yield this year, alongside a warning that aging infrastructure and rising cybersecurity risks will likely produce more such disruptions. Reflecting on a recent meeting with President Trump and other CEOs focused on declining international inbound travel, which is down roughly twelve million visitors versus 2019 despite the World Cup, it was argued that tourism lacks the cabinet-level representation it holds in other major economies, despite contributing about 3% of U.S. GDP and over 15 million jobs, and that better balancing security and openness could improve outcomes for inbound visitors.

## Key Takeaways

### 💰 Prices are set by customer value, not simply by costs

Carnival does not automatically pass higher fuel, labor or food costs on to customers. It prices according to what consumers are willing to pay, with cost changes affecting the gap between revenue and profit rather than directly determining fares.

Quotes:

> We never price based on cost. We don’t. We price based on what the consumer is willing to pay. We don’t pass things on. That’s not our business model.

### 🌍 U.S. consumers now prioritize travel more like European consumers

Since the pandemic, travel has become a much higher priority for U.S. consumers, resembling the long-standing European practice of protecting holiday spending.

Quotes:

> The prioritization that the U.S. consumer gives to travel... has finally caught up, frankly, where Europe has been for decades.

### 🧳 Booking strategy must span advisors and digital channels

Cruise companies need to serve travelers according to their preferred booking method, preserving relationships with trusted travel advisors while supporting growing direct digital demand.

Quotes:

> We need to meet our consumer wherever they’re going to be and however they want to book. There are some people that, for the medium term certainly, are going to want to talk to a person, a trusted advisor... But more and more... people [are] more used to going digital booking directly into our own websites.

### 🌐 Inbound travel needs a stronger place in U.S. policymaking

The U.S. travel industry is urging Washington to treat tourism as a strategic economic sector and balance security measures with a more welcoming approach to international visitors. The speaker links inbound travel to roughly 3% of GDP and more than 15 million jobs, while noting that the country will receive 12 million fewer international visitors than in 2019.

Quotes:

> In every major economy in the world, tourism has a cabinet seat around the table. The only place it doesn't is here... Security is critical and so is tourism.

### ⚠️ Shocks delay travel bookings, but demand can recover strongly

Economic, geopolitical and operational shocks can temporarily delay bookings while consumers assess their jobs, savings and future. Once uncertainty eases, demand can normalize quickly; Carnival reported that recent disruptions cost it several hundred million dollars in potential revenue, but only a few points of yield rather than a pandemic-scale collapse.

Quotes:

> People just take a little time to normalize... But even then, people got past it and they normalized... and started booking en masse again.

### ⚙️ AI is becoming an operating-efficiency tool

Carnival is applying AI beyond customer-facing distribution, using it across functions to improve efficiency, generate demand and strengthen revenue performance. The company says tangible benefits have emerged rapidly after an initially exploratory phase.

Quotes:

> We are seeing and utilizing AI in ways up and down our P&L and across our functions to find efficiency. To generate more demand and be better at the revenue generation. And it’s remarkable how quickly it is coming to fruition.

### 🛫 Aging infrastructure is making travel disruptions more frequent

Travel companies should expect more recurring operational shocks as newer technologies are layered onto aging aviation and travel infrastructure. These disruptions are manageable but can materially reduce revenue, and cybersecurity adds another fast-moving risk as bad actors adopt AI tools.

Quotes:

> It does seem like the type of technology we're trying to introduce into our systems is not compatible with the age and infrastructure of what exists. And so there's going to be these types of disruptions.

### ⛽ Efficiency is Carnival’s main response to volatile input costs

Rather than hedge fuel prices, Carnival focuses on consuming less fuel, food, water and other resources. The company says fuel consumption per unit is down 26% versus 2019, while absolute fuel use is down 13% despite being 11% larger.

Quotes:

> We focused on using less. If you consume less, you save money. And so we are maniacal about consumption. Everything from fuel to food, to the beverages to water production—how do we do things more efficiently and effectively all the time?

### 🚢 Cruising has resilience as a relative-value vacation

Cruise demand may be less sensitive to downturns because cruises compare favorably on price with other vacation alternatives.

Quotes:

> The great thing about cruising is we’re a relative value. So we have some level of resistance to economic downturns because when you comp us versus other vacation alternatives, we’re cheaper.

### 🤖 AI is moving travel distribution toward digital assistants

AI is disrupting advisor-led, direct and website-based booking models. Cruise companies must remain discoverable and useful within AI platforms, as consumers may increasingly rely on digital assistants to plan and purchase travel rather than visiting websites themselves.

Quotes:

> I don’t know what the over-under on number of years is, but it’s single digits, I think, that people won’t be going to websites at all. Because they’re going to pull out their phone... and have their digital assistant literally do everything for them.
