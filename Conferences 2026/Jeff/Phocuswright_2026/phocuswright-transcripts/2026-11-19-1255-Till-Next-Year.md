## Summary

Reflecting on the hardest lesson of the past year, the Google guest observed that the pace of change in AI may be outstripping the travel ecosystem's ability to absorb it, pushing user behavior in surprising new directions — a disruption that, while unsettling, also creates opportunity. Both speakers agreed that the sense of things moving "too fast" is unlikely to go away anytime soon.

Asked how a hotel or OTA CEO should allocate next year's search and discovery budget, the response emphasized a portfolio approach grounded first in factual accuracy and depth of property content, since large language models now stitch together granular details — pillow quality, hallway noise, bar atmosphere — that traditional search never surfaced. The bulk of investment should go toward accurate pricing, availability, technical feed plumbing, and standout photos and human voice on owned websites, with a secondary allocation toward being "agentic-ready," and roughly 10% reserved for speculative, far-out projects.

On the value exchange between Google and travel partners as more research and decision-making happens inside AI-driven search, it was stressed that the underlying physics of travel — booking flights, staying under a roof, ending the journey with a partner — haven't changed, but partners need more context about why and how a user arrives from Google. Currently, no prior search or conversational context is passed to a hotel when a booking happens inside AI mode, though this was described as an evolving area that must always be built on user consent and control rather than silent data sharing, since real continuity of the journey between Google and partner experiences requires more transparency.

On trust, as ads scale within AI mode, the commitment was to keep labeling clear and consistent with existing search practices and to explain to users why a recommendation appeared — whether from a loyalty brand, a deal, or a sponsored placement — even though this takes up cognitive space and will require ongoing research into how well users actually understand these distinctions. Core search principles were reaffirmed as commercially neutral: ranking and surfacing of content are not influenced by commercial relationships, and that same neutrality must extend to how large language models compose trip plans, even as "ranking" takes a different form in an AI-generated answer.

On agentic booking and supplier control — raised in light of United's decision to block agents from shopping and booking on its site — suppliers were said to deserve choice in how they participate, though the lines get blurry in a browser-based agentic world versus an API-based one, especially when determining whether an agent or the user ultimately completes a purchase at a confirmation step; responsibility for that transaction has to be shared. Pushed on whether Google's shift to AI answers is cannibalizing its own search business, the reply framed the shift as simply following changing user needs and expectations rather than resisting them. Closing out, priorities for 2027 were summarized as getting it right for users, getting it right for partners, and doing so even faster than today. The session closed with organizational remarks thanking sponsors and attendees, noting conference statistics captured via SnapSite, and previewing upcoming events including the Travel Marketing AI Summit in New York and London, Phocuswright Europe in London, WIT events worldwide, a new event in Buenos Aires, and a return to Queenstown in 2028, alongside thanks to the Phocuswright team and parent company NorthStar/JTB.
