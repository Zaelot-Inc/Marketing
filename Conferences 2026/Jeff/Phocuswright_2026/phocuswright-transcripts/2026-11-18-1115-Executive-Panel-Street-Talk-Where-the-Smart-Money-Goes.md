## Summary

The panel opened with each investor naming where their next dollar would go: one favored the distribution space, another named Amazon as a public-markets pick with a derivative read-through from Nvidia and travel IPO activity, while also flagging Airbnb and a 'dislocated high-quality' thesis on Booking.com given easier year-ahead comps and overstated agentic disintermediation risk. A fourth panelist said they wanted to back a platform providing identity for agentic commerce.

Asked what could go wrong, the panel converged on fears around a single dominant horizontal AI agent capturing most of the economic value and squeezing OTA advertising revenue and bookings share, which was framed as bad not just for the industry but for humanity broadly; another risk cited was losing the human element of hospitality as AI scales, with a caution that AI should support rather than replace human connection, while a third flagged consumer adoption patterns as the wildcard that could disrupt projected ripple effects across the industry. On readiness, there was agreement that average consumers aren't yet ready to trust AI with a several-thousand-dollar annual vacation purchase, but that this will change within a year or two as AI takes over discrete 'jobs to be done' like itinerary optimization rather than the entire trip.

On what not to fund, voice AI was singled out as a commodity lacking a defensible moat, while another panelist said they'd avoid any company failing to adopt agentic AI, drawing a parallel to the slow but inevitable shift of travel bookings online decades ago and warning that travel companies must become AI-native the way they once had to become internet companies. On where capital is actually flowing, panelists described near-universal AI-native sourcing, a pivot from loyalty, distribution and search toward labor-enhancing 'operations technology' aimed at absorbing hospitality labor tasks (with hoteliers still questioning near-term ROI), and a split focus on 'picks and shovels' for agentic commerce such as identity and content syndication, alongside non-AI-disruptable product categories like a differentiated travel insurance offering, with operational excellence underscored as the real driver of repeat travel.

On public markets, the view was that a travel IPO could succeed only with a genuinely differentiated, high-growth, AI-enabled value proposition, echoing Airbnb's 2020 debut, since public investors are starved for growth and chasing names like Anthropic. OpenAI's acquisition of Odessia split opinion: one panelist saw it as validating but ultimately a negative signal for independent travel-AI startups, expressing far greater concern about Meta's Muse, which is free, preloaded across billions of accounts, and avoids the cold-start problem; another argued Odessia validates travel's outsized share of LLM queries even without a travel backbone, drawing parallels to Google's earlier struggles integrating hotels versus flights. A show of hands indicated notable existing Muse usage, and one panelist voiced strong conviction that only two or three companies (Google, Meta, possibly OpenAI) have the scale and ad-funded economics to make mass-market AI travel planning work, eventually turning into customer acquisition channels that OTAs—strong in supply aggregation and channel optimization—could monetize. Another panelist pushed back that current tools like Muse and Instinct remain slow and unreliable for actual hotel search, attributing this to both early growing pains and immature plumbing, and a further nuance offered was that the future likely involves AI 'swarms' of specialized vertical agents working with horizontal ones rather than a single super-agent.

On whether incumbent OTAs can survive this shift, the consensus distinguished between the consumer-facing .com businesses, seen as structurally threatened because personal AI agents will know travelers better than any aggregator site, and the underlying B2B supply-aggregation businesses, viewed as far more durable and likely to persist as logos even as front-end power shifts toward whoever owns consumer context. Hotels were seen as having a genuine opening to reclaim distribution by leaning on the on-the-ground delivery of experience and personalization that resellers can only promise, turning context and direct relationships into the new loyalty currency. On the broader growth outlook, online travel was judged to have matured into a GDP-plus growth category, with continued OTA advantage resting on the deep fragmentation of global hotel supply and OTAs' rich historical travel data, which a closing lightning round reinforced: AI trip planners were called a 'nice feature' rather than a standalone business, opinions split on who wins the agentic race between startups, OTAs, LLMs and suppliers, and B2C trip planning was flagged as the most overfunded current travel theme.

## Key Takeaways

### 🧭 AI trip planners may be features, not standalone businesses

The panel sees near-term value in using AI for tedious travel optimization, such as complex itineraries, but the lightning-round consensus was that an AI trip planner alone is more likely to be a feature than a durable standalone business.

Quotes:

> “AI trip planner. Nice feature or real business?... Feature. It breaks my heart, but it's a feature.”

### 🌐 Scaled horizontal AI platforms could control travel’s top funnel

The panel sees Meta, Google and potentially OpenAI as uniquely positioned to make AI agents widely accessible through massive user bases and advertising-funded infrastructure. These agents could weaken OTA consumer brands while becoming important customer-acquisition channels for OTA supply.

Quotes:

> “The top of the funnel will be owned by the people with context... Meta and whatever Google comes out with and OpenAI, they'll eventually become customer acquisition channels.”

### 🏨 Operational excellence is a core travel differentiator

The panel argues that operations are not merely a back-office concern: execution quality determines whether travelers want to repeat an experience. This makes operational improvement an important investment area even when it appears less exciting than consumer-facing technology.

Quotes:

> “Operational excellence is the difference between a trip you want to go on again and a trip you don't want to go on again.”

### 🏗️ OTA consumer brands and B2B platforms face different risks

The panel separates an OTA’s consumer-facing website from its underlying B2B inventory aggregation and booking infrastructure. AI agents may make brands such as Expedia.com and Booking.com less important for recommendations, while still relying on their supply, connectivity and booking capabilities.

Quotes:

> “The OTA has two large businesses within... There is a dot-com consumer aggregator business... And there is a B2B inventory aggregation business below it. I actually think that the dot-com business is in a pretty bad place. I think the aggregation business [is] in a much stronger place.”

### 🏨 Hotels can use agentic distribution to reclaim customer context

The panel sees agentic commerce as a once-in-a-generation opportunity for hotels and other suppliers to build direct relationships around customer context and personalization. Suppliers still have the advantage in delivering the actual on-the-ground experience, even if intermediaries promise it.

Quotes:

> “Context is becoming increasingly more relevant. That's the new way of loyalty... There's an opportunity to gain more information and context.”

### 🛠️ Invest in agentic picks-and-shovels and AI-resilient businesses

Rather than trying to predict the eventual shape of agentic distribution, investors can back enabling infrastructure such as identity and content syndication, alongside businesses whose core value is not itself easily disrupted by AI. These positions may remain relevant regardless of which platforms ultimately win.

Quotes:

> “The two things I'm investing in are picks and shovels for agentic commerce... And then I'm investing in things that AI cannot disrupt, or AI is not the core of it.”

### 📊 Online travel is mature but still has GDP-plus growth potential

The panel views online travel as a mature category rather than an exhausted one, expecting GDP-plus growth over time. Fragmented lodging markets, particularly outside the United States, and OTAs’ accumulated traveler data remain structural sources of opportunity.

Quotes:

> “Online travel is clearly matured. You probably have GDP-plus growth for the category... just how freaking fragmented the hotel market is.”

### 🚀 Travel companies must build agentic functionality

The panel compares agentic adoption with the earlier shift to online booking: companies that fail to incorporate agentic functionality into their apps risk becoming structurally disadvantaged as consumer behavior changes.

Quotes:

> “You have to adopt it. You have to have that functionality on your app as part of your business. If you don't, I wouldn't invest in them.”

### 🎙️ Voice AI needs a moat beyond the commodity layer

The panel’s strongest avoid recommendation was voice AI as a standalone investment theme: basic voice capabilities are widely available, so companies need a differentiated moat beyond the interface itself.

Quotes:

> “Voice AI... is a commodity. Anyone can build with voice AI. Find a different moat.”

### 🐝 AI swarms may outperform a single super-agent

Rather than relying on one agent to handle every travel task, the panel expects groups of specialized agents to work together. A general-purpose agent could coordinate vertical travel agents with deeper domain knowledge and infrastructure.

Quotes:

> “I'm a big believer in AI swarms. Groups of AI agents that come together to accomplish overall things.”
