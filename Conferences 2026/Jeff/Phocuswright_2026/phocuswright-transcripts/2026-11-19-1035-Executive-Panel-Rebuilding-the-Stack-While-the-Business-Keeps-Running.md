## Summary

The panel opened on how companies are allocating investment given the pace of change in the industry. One executive described shifting spend toward an efficient API and orchestration layer built on top of a newly migrated platform, with investment now focused on helping agency partners cope with agentic search disrupting their systems and become more competitive against agentic players. Another executive explained that traditional 3-to-5-year strategic planning has given way to 3-to-6-month horizons, since topics considered irrelevant six months ago are now mainstream; the focus is on positioning the company as 'intelligent supply' so customers can participate in evolving demand, whatever the latest model or demand-side partnership turns out to be.

Asked what else the industry needs to upgrade legacy infrastructure for agentic commerce, panelists pointed to three unresolved issues: poorly cataloged, unstructured data that blocks true e-commerce readiness; payments friction, since agents still trip over reCAPTCHA and similar checkpoints even when going through connectors; and governance, specifically how to distinguish helpful agents from scraping bots that companies want blocked. A related tension was raised around whether hoteliers even want agentic bookings at all, given concerns that agents won't upsell the way human-assisted bookings do, suggesting the choice may end up being made hotel-by-hotel rather than dictated industry-wide.

One executive argued that the bigger obstacle isn't legacy technology but organizational structure. The company had already completed a full end-to-end agentic booking including payment, but volume hasn't scaled because basic accountability questions remain unanswered: who is responsible if an agent's performance or payment fails, and how disruptions like airspace closures and rebookings get handled. The conclusion was that the technology to complete agentic bookings already exists, but organizations are not yet ready to maneuver around the operational and accountability questions it raises.

On model selection, the view was that there is no single right choice between open and closed source or local versus cloud deployment; it depends on the specific problem, since different models excel at different tasks, and the value of platform vendors lies in helping pick the right model for a given use case, such as choosing between models for complex pricing decisions. When asked what the industry could learn from a recent FAA incident about rebuilding infrastructure, the quick responses were pointed but light—hire the right contractor, don't use copper wire—acknowledging the comparison to running a company is imperfect but that the stakes and consequences of infrastructure choices are real.

Looking ahead one year, panelists expected incremental but real progress. One expects continued franchisee-focused product investment, helped by data that is already well-structured. Another noted that while industry survey data on AI adoption hasn't moved much in the past year, both more progress and a continually moving finish line will be true simultaneously. A third predicted more authentic support for travel agents as automation frees up their time for client-facing work while lowering servicing costs.

## Key Takeaways

### 🤖 Prepare agencies for agentic search

Travel businesses need to help agencies handle the operational impact of agentic search while improving their efficiency enough to compete with agentic systems.

Quotes:

> We invest quite a lot of time and money in helping our agencies to overcome the problems that they will start to see when agentic search starts punching into their systems. We also need to make them more efficient to be able to compete with agentic systems out there.

### 🤝 Redirect agents toward higher-value client work

As AI automates more travel-agent processes, the expected benefit is not only lower servicing costs but also more time for agents to support clients directly.

Quotes:

> A lot of processes will get automated. It will free up time for the agents to spend more time with clients, and it will at the same time actually lower the cost of servicing clients.

### 🧩 Match the model to the problem

There is no universal choice between open and closed-source or local and cloud models. The appropriate approach depends on the problem, while vendors and platforms can help companies combine models for different requirements such as complex pricing decisions.

Quotes:

> I think it really depends on the situation of the problem that you're looking to solve. Different models are good at different things.

### 🏗️ Build innovation on top of the migrated platform

Kenneth Johansen said the immediate investment focus is no longer migration itself, but using the new platform’s API and process-orchestration layer to bring innovation and greater efficiency to agencies.

Quotes:

> We spent time on migrating to the new platform. We're building a very efficient API and process orchestration layer on top of that platform. So now it's for us about bringing innovation to our agencies.

### 🏨 Weigh agentic bookings against lost upsells

Hotels are divided over whether to enable agentic booking: it may generate additional sales and add-on revenue, but agents may not accept the upsells that many hotels rely on. Participation could become a property-level decision.

Quotes:

> Do they want the additional sales and add-on revenue to be limited as an agent might not be upsold, like so many hoteliers count on?

### 🏢 Prepare the organization for failures, not just transactions

End-to-end agentic booking, including payment, is technically possible, but companies still need clear ownership for agent performance, failed payments, disruptions and rebooking. Organizational readiness is now a larger constraint than basic transaction capability.

Quotes:

> The technology is actually already there to complete an agent booking. But from an organizational standpoint, I'm not really sure we are ready to really maneuver.

### 💳 Remove payment and authentication friction

Agentic commerce depends on payment and authentication flows that agents can complete; mechanisms such as reCAPTCHA still cause agents to fail during transactions.

Quotes:

> We don't talk about [payments] enough... you still have to go through reCAPTCHA. And agents trip up on reCAPTCHA.

### 🔄 Plan in short cycles as the market shifts

With travel technology changing faster than traditional three- or five-year plans can accommodate, companies are focusing on three- to six-month horizons while continuously preparing themselves and customers for what comes next.

Quotes:

> Things that we did not think were relevant or possible 6 months ago are now common topics of conversation. So we're consistently looking at what's next, how is it evolving, and how can we make ourselves and our customers ready for that evolution.

### 🛡️ Govern which agents can transact

Companies need scalable, business-specific rules for deciding which automated traffic to allow. Blanket bot blocking can prevent useful commerce agents from reaching systems, while unwanted scraping and other costly or harmful activity may still need to be stopped. Participation may ultimately vary by hotel or business.

Quotes:

> How do we let in the things that our customers want to participate in, and how do we block a lot of the activity that could drive up our costs or could have adverse outcomes?

### 📚 Structure catalogs before commerce can scale

A major infrastructure gap is that much of the industry’s data is not cataloged in a structured way, limiting the ability of agents and other systems to conduct commerce reliably.

Quotes:

> A lot of the data out there is still not cataloged really well. So for e-commerce or commerce to really take off, you need to have a structured catalog.
