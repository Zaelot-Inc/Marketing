## Summary

The session opened with the induction of this year's Phocuswright Hall of Fame honoree, who began his travel career opening more than 250 restaurants for PepsiCo before joining a young Expedia, where he managed hotel partnerships worth over $2 billion a year in gross bookings. He later spent 16 years as Managing Director of Travel at Google working with major airlines, hotel companies, OTAs and destinations, then returned to Expedia Group to run and more than double the revenue of its advertising business into a billion-dollar-plus media network. His industry leadership also included chairing HSMAI, serving as Vice Chairman of the World Travel and Tourism Council, and sitting on the US Travel and Tourism Advisory Board and the board of Global Glimpse.

The main interview opened on a personal note, with the Expedia B2B leader recounting traveling to the US with his son to watch seven World Cup matches in seven cities, continuing a tradition his own father had started with him in 1982, and deliberately skipping the final to prioritize sharing the tournament experience with his family. Asked how the Spanish national team's success reflected his approach to building Expedia's B2B business, he described it as a team effort rather than a collection of star players, crediting colleagues across M&A, supply, and product teams—including those behind recent acquisitions such as CarTrawler—for enabling the business to integrate new capabilities and achieve velocity. He also emphasized that having teams distributed across markets such as Madrid, Dublin, London, Seattle and Singapore helps the company understand local partner and traveler needs, since every segment and every country differs.

On the pressures facing B2B travel, the discussion turned to why banks, airlines and retailers are entering travel: it was framed as a natural expansion into a category that generates significant consumer spend, with these companies relying on technology and servicing partners like Expedia while they focus on their core customer relationships. Asked how Expedia's B2B unit compares to priorities Booking Holdings has articulated—speed, customization and simplicity—he argued that speed and execution have long been givens for his team, and that trust in partner relationships will become even more important as AI adoption grows. He noted that recent acquisitions such as CarTrawler and whole-ticket activities deals were pursued because building equivalent capabilities internally would have taken too long, and that once these tools are fully integrated by around the second half of 2027, the company expects to reach full velocity, already launching partners roughly 75% faster than before. A live audience poll on what will matter most for B2B platforms showed customization and simplicity as the top choices, with the executive agreeing that customization is critical since partners want genuine differentiation rather than templated look-alikes.

On the question of power, there was no ambiguity that partners, not platforms, own the traveler relationship. This created little conflict for Expedia because the company deliberately concentrated its own B2C business on the US during COVID while leaving Asia Pacific and Europe growth to partners and B2B, unlike Booking.com which has large direct consumer presence in those regions. On whether AI makes it easy for new entrants to build their own booking products and bypass incumbents like Expedia, the response was that creating a basic booking flow is simple, but travel's complexity—especially servicing issues such as the recent US air traffic control disruptions—requires mastery that is hard to replicate, and that incumbents' operational depth will ultimately be underestimated by newer, noisier entrants.

The conversation closed by looking at a pivot in business models, illustrated through the expanded Qantas partnership. Moving beyond traditional white-label templates that often looked indistinguishable from Expedia or Booking.com, the company built a composable, agentic, AI-powered front end that cut launch times from three to four months down to three to four days. With Qantas specifically, Expedia now also powers the technology behind Qantas Holidays' directly negotiated hotel contracts with chains like Accor, Hilton and Marriott, earning a transaction fee while letting Qantas keep those commercial relationships and focus its marketing on flights—an arrangement described as a long-held ambition that opens new B2B opportunities. A rapid-fire closing round touched on travel sellers' biggest pressure being the generally volatile, fast-changing macro environment rather than one single factor, a preference for Formula 1 over soccer and cash-and-points over pure cash on personal trips, a view that agentic booking is overhyped short-term but underhyped long-term, and interest in extending Expedia's technology to power other consumer brands such as football clubs (noting an existing relationship with Real Madrid) or universities.

## Key Takeaways

### ⚡ Acquisitions accelerate launches only after integration

Expedia is acquiring capabilities in areas such as car rental, activities and experiences instead of building them entirely in-house. The speed benefit depends on integrating the acquired technology and creating synergies; once integrated, Expedia expects to launch partners 75% faster than before.

Quotes:

> We are able now with all this technology to launch partners 75% faster than what we used to do before. So once you integrate in all of that, things get easier. Until then, it's a little bit rough.

### 🔐 Trust remains critical alongside AI

As AI and automation become more prominent in travel, trusted relationships with partners will become even more important rather than less.

Quotes:

> We were talking about AI, we were talking about machines and so on and so on, but trust relationships are the other ones that are going to be key now.

### 🎯 Execution and integration drive B2B growth

Torres credits Expedia B2B’s progress to a strong, complementary team, but emphasizes that execution is what turns acquisitions and strategy into growth. Integrating companies, creating synergies and maintaining the existing business are central to the approach.

Quotes:

> I will put my focus on execution... You need to maintain all of that, you need to execute, you need to bring all the companies together and have synergies.

### 👥 Partners, not platforms, own the traveler relationship

In B2B travel, Expedia’s role is to enable partners that own the customer relationship, while helping them differentiate through customized products and services.

Quotes:

> Who owns the traveler? Our partners. That's for sure. There is no discussion.

### 🤝 Specialist partners let new travel sellers outsource complexity

Organizations entering travel can focus on their customer relationships and core strengths while specialist partners provide the technology, servicing and operational expertise required to deliver travel products.

Quotes:

> They are letting others helping them... while we take care of the technology servicing, et cetera, et cetera. So it's a win-win.

### 🧩 Platforms can power a partner’s own supplier relationships

Expedia’s expanded Qantas model goes beyond supplying inventory: Qantas keeps its direct hotel relationships while Expedia powers the underlying technology and earns a transaction fee. This lets the airline focus its resources on selling flights and marketing its core business.

Quotes:

> They’re going to keep on those relationships with Accor, Hilton, Marriott, et cetera. But the technology, we’re also going to power that technology where we operate getting a transaction fee.

### 🌍 Local presence helps Expedia understand varied travel needs

A team distributed across markets gives Expedia B2B closer insight into partners, customers and travelers, whose needs differ by country and segment.

Quotes:

> You need to be in the market. You need to understand the needs of your partners, your customers, to understand what is it that the travelers want.

### 🎨 Customization makes B2B travel products distinctive

Travel sellers want products that feel distinctive rather than generic white-label storefronts. Expedia’s composable template, with an AI-powered front end, is designed to provide that customization while reducing launch time from three or four months to three or four days.

Quotes:

> We create this composable agentic template... What it used to take us 3 months, 4 months to launch per point of sale, we do it in 3, 4 days now.

### 🤖 Agentic booking has a long-term opportunity despite short-term hype

Torres expects agentic booking to be overhyped in the short term but underhyped in the long term, suggesting travel sellers should distinguish near-term excitement from the technology’s longer-term potential.

Quotes:

> Overhyped in the short term, underhyped in the long term.

### 🛎️ Servicing complexity remains a travel-industry moat

Building a basic hotel-booking interface is relatively easy, but handling disruptions, customer service, complex reservation chains and packages requires specialized operational capabilities that AI and new entrants may struggle to replicate.

Quotes:

> Travel is much more complex... Who is taking care of that? AI? No. Customer service. And you need to have that. You need to master that. That is hard to replicate.
