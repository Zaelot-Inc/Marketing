## Summary

The session opened with a sponsor presentation on Crewfare, a platform built around the idea that the ticket, not the hotel, is now the strongest signal in travel. Crewfare argued that once a fan buys a ticket to an event like Formula 1 Miami or Lollapalooza, they are currently left to plan the rest of the trip alone, and that hotels misprice rooms around event dates while losing revenue and data because nobody tracks where ticket buyers actually come from or what they would have booked. Crewfare's product suite integrates hotels, tickets, transportation and activities at the point of ticket purchase, working directly with leagues, promoters and festivals so travel is part of the experience from the first click rather than something fans figure out afterward, with examples including Lollapalooza, Ultra and NHL events.

The main research presentation, drawn from new Phocuswright Consumer Travel and Travel Advisor survey data, set the scene with three numbers: 52% of US travelers used AI for trip planning in the past year, 21% called or visited an advisor to book at least one trip component, and 73% of advisors now use GenAI themselves, up from 59% a year earlier. The core finding was that AI sits at the top of the planning funnel, sparking slightly more trip ideas than advisors (7% versus 5%) and running neck-and-neck with advisors in research (21% versus 23%), but its role collapses at booking, where AI accounts for only about 2% of actual bookings versus roughly 21% for advisors, even though 95% of AI users said it influenced at least one trip decision. AI was described as functioning more as a copilot than a booking channel.

Four common industry assumptions were tested and each failed to hold. First, AI is not pulling travelers away from advisors; advisor clients actually use AI more and trust it more, but are also more likely to say it falls short for specific or advanced needs, meaning advisor clients are experienced AI users who have hit its limits. Second, advisor clients are not simply older travelers: advisors perceive 45% of clients as 56+, but traveler data shows only 25%, since younger travelers book flights and hotels through advisors in small, easily-overlooked transactions while older cruisers dominate advisor revenue and perception. Third, booking is not moving inside AI any time soon: 43% of AI-familiar travelers say they would book inside an AI platform, but only about 2% (rising to roughly 8% once Google's AI mode is included) actually did, with travelers still preferring OTAs and direct supplier sites because of accountability if something goes wrong. Fourth, trust is not an advisor-only advantage; overall trust in AI and advisors is tied at 38%, but this masks a generational split, with Gen Z and millennials trusting AI more and Gen X and boomers favoring advisors.

On the advisor side, GenAI adoption has climbed sharply, from 41% in 2024 to 73% this year, led by newer advisors with five years or less experience, who are far more eager to expand AI use than those with 20+ years in the business. Advisors overwhelmingly want AI for marketing content and itinerary building rather than client-facing tools; only 17% want to offer clients a chatbot, and two-thirds of agency owners have no plans to do so, preferring to remain the face of the relationship. The main payoff reported is time saved on admin and personalization, though a third of advisors, and half of those with 20+ years, say AI has not yet meaningfully changed their work, and many report spending time correcting client-brought AI misinformation. Both advisors and travelers draw a similar line: comfort with AI monitoring bookings in the background is high, but willingness to let AI book or change a reservation unsupervised is very low (3% of advisors, with travelers split roughly 37% yes to 39% no).

The presentation closed by flagging how fast the landscape is shifting even since the surveys closed, with Google, Meta, Expedia and Booking Holdings all adding AI booking features that nonetheless still route responsibility through a known brand or person, matching what travelers and advisors say they want. The recommended takeaways were that advisors should optimize for AI-driven search, court younger travelers who already book with them, and position themselves to validate and improve AI-built plans rather than compete with them; OTAs and suppliers should aim to be the trusted place travelers complete bookings after AI research; and travel tech should build AI that assists and then hands off to a person or brand. The overarching conclusion was that AI and people both help travelers decide, often on the same trip, with AI shaping the plan and people still making the booking, and that the gap between the 43% who say they would book inside AI and the 2% who actually do is the key metric to watch for when that balance shifts.

## Key Takeaways

### ✅ Human approval remains the boundary for autonomous travel AI

Both advisors and travelers are more comfortable with AI researching, assisting and monitoring trips than independently booking or changing them. Accuracy, personal touch and accountability concerns make approval at the point of purchase a persistent requirement.

Quotes:

> On both sides, AI is welcome to research and assist. People still want to approve the booking.

### 📣 Advisors must show up where AI starts planning

Because AI now sparks more trip ideas than advisors, advisors should optimize for AI-driven discovery and prepare to improve plans clients bring from AI. Validating and personalizing those plans can help advisors earn the booking instead of competing with the initial recommendation.

Quotes:

> The advisors who validate and improve upon that plan instead of competing with it, will earn the booking.

### 🛡️ Responsibility remains a booking barrier for AI

Although 43% of travelers familiar with AI say they would book inside an AI platform, current behavior is much lower because travelers want clarity about who is responsible when something goes wrong. That unresolved trust and accountability gap is central to future distribution shifts.

Quotes:

> AI shapes the decision, and then the travelers leave to book somewhere they already know and trust. Online travel agencies are still the most common way to book flights and hotels, followed directly through suppliers.

### 📊 Disconnected booking obscures valuable traveler data

When organizers and suppliers do not connect ticket and travel behavior, they may not know who attended, where they came from or what additional products they would have purchased.

Quotes:

> Nobody knows who came, from where, and what they would have bought unless somebody asked.

### 🤖 AI is moving to the top of the travel funnel

AI increasingly shapes trip ideas and research before travelers reach supplier or OTA sites: 52% of U.S. travelers used AI for trips in the past year, while 21% used it to research trips—up from 6% two years ago. Businesses need to be visible during this early decision-making stage.

Quotes:

> AI now sits at the very top of the funnel, shaping shortlists before travelers reach your site or app, and it’s moving fast.

### 🏨 Events create major revenue swings for hotels

Hotels that price only from normal seasonal patterns can miss the demand created by major events. Event-linked pricing and distribution can capture substantially higher rates when demand changes the economics of a destination.

Quotes:

> When an event came to the city that warranted the price difference, hotels were not priced at $200 a night. They were priced at $600, $800, $1,000.

### 💳 AI influences decisions more than it closes bookings

Travelers frequently act on AI recommendations, but actual booking behavior remains far behind stated interest: 95% of AI users made at least one trip decision based on AI, while only about 2% said AI was their booking channel—roughly 8% when Google AI Mode is included. Travelers still leave AI to book with OTAs or suppliers they know.

Quotes:

> 95% of AI users made at least one trip decision based on what AI told them... But only 9% said AI was the tool they relied on most. So it's really kind of just a copilot.

### ⚙️ Advisors want AI to improve the work behind the relationship

Advisors are most interested in AI for emails, marketing, website content, itineraries and package curation—not for replacing the advisor in client conversations. The opportunity is to automate routine work while preserving the advisor as the face of the relationship.

Quotes:

> Advisors want AI for marketing, and itineraries, far more than for client-facing chatbots.

### 🧭 Younger travelers are an underdeveloped advisor opportunity

Advisor businesses may overestimate how old their clients are: only 25% of travelers who booked through an advisor are 55 or older, versus advisors’ estimate that 45% of clients are 56-plus. Younger travelers already use advisors for some products, but Gen Z and millennials trust AI more, so advisors must actively build credibility with them.

Quotes:

> Younger travelers are already walking through the door, most advisors are just not building a business around them yet.

### 🤝 Advisors and AI are emerging as collaborators, not replacements

AI has not displaced travel advisors: 21% of travelers used an advisor to book at least one trip component, 23% used one for research, and 73% of advisors now use GenAI, up from 59% a year earlier. AI increasingly shapes the plan while advisors validate, personalize and take responsibility for the booking.

Quotes:

> The answer is both AI and people. And very often it's for the same trip. AI shapes the plan, people still make the booking.

### 🎟️ Build the trip around the event ticket

The ticket is a powerful signal of a traveler’s destination, timing and intent. Event organizers and travel partners can use it to integrate hotels, transportation and activities from the first click, rather than leaving fans to assemble the trip afterward.

Quotes:

> We start where the fans do, at the ticket... Travel is an integrated part of the experience from the very first click, not something that the fan has to figure out afterward.
