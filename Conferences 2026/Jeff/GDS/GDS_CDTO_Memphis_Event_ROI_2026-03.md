## Funnel

| Contacts met | → Lead | → SQL | → Customer | SQLs w/ brief | Cost per SQL |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **10** | 2 | **6** | 1 | 4 of 6 | **$7,332** |

Read against the room: 102 attendee records on the CDTO March master sheet, 64 with contact detail on file, **11 slots booked and 9 attended**, plus two unscheduled floor conversations. That is a 9.8% contact rate against the full attendee pool.

**One booked meeting was a no-show.** Emanuela Delgado (SVP Growth & Innovation, Parts Town) held the 3:40 PM slot on 3/11 and did not attend, confirmed by her absence from the 64-contact sheet and by her own brief ("missed you in Memphis"). Parts Town remains a live GDS-sourced relationship with a brief on file, but it was not sourced at this conference and is excluded from every figure here.

**Three of the ten contacts lost their roles.** Susan DiGregorio and Ali Heriyanto both had their positions at The Corcoran Group eliminated before the trip, and Nathan Kimpel was let go from Cushman & Wakefield promptly after the conference, before the follow-up call could happen. That is a **30% role-loss rate on a single conference cohort**, and it is the single most useful planning fact in this report.

**Corcoran shows that multi-threading alone is not the remedy.** Two Corcoran people were met back to back, DiGregorio at 4:50 PM and Heriyanto at 6:00 PM, and both roles were gone. Two doors into one account, both closed, because both sat in the same product and technology function during what looks like a restructuring of it. The account is not partially damaged, it is gone.

The usable version of the lesson is narrower than "book two slots." It is that two contacts from the same function at the same company are one point of failure wearing two badges. Coverage has to span functions or levels to be worth anything, and at Corcoran it did not.

Unit economics at $43,991 all in:

| Metric | Cost |
|---|---|
| Per contact met (10) | $4,399 |
| Per viable contact (7, excluding three disqualified) | $6,284 |
| Per SQL (6) | **$7,332** |
| Per customer won (1) | $43,991, against $158,000 of booked revenue |
| Per account in the CRM, including the 10 co-sell accounts seeded from intake (15) | $2,933 |

$7,332 per SQL is defensible for accounts in the $100K to $5M band. The whole trip cost less than a third of the one deal it closed.

**A definitional note that matters.** No contact in this cohort sits at HubSpot's `salesqualifiedlead` stage. Everyone here went from `lead` straight to `opportunity`, so "SQL" in this report means `lifecyclestage = opportunity`, which is also what the `SQL_Galusha/` folder convention tracks.

The stage itself is not unused portal-wide. At least one contact elsewhere sits at `salesqualifiedlead` (Brian Demarest, PistenBully, from the NSAA circuit). So this is not a disabled stage, it is an inconsistently applied one: the same commercial reality is recorded as `opportunity` on one rep's accounts and `salesqualifiedlead` on another's. Any cross-rep or cross-event funnel comparison is unreliable until that is standardized, and it should be settled before anyone reports SQL counts to leadership.

## What this says about event strategy

Memphis is the counter-case to the read in the 2026 event recap, and now it has the receipt. That document argues the niche shows underperform on prospect pool, which pushed the year toward bigger general conferences. Memphis is a curated 1:1 summit, not a trade show: **$43,991 in, $158,000 booked, a new customer logo, six SQLs at $7,332 each**, and ten co-sell accounts seeded from the intake data at $2,933 per account in the CRM. Ten senior conversations in three days plus a 102-record intake dataset. Nothing else on the 2026 calendar did that.

The unresolved problem is not the event, it is what happened after it. The return came from one of ten contacts. Four of six deals closed lost, one is fourteen weeks past its close date, and the ten co-sell accounts pulled from the intake list have sat untouched for five months. A 3.6x return on a trip where we worked maybe a fifth of what it produced is an argument for going back and doing the follow-through properly, not evidence that we ran it well.

The NSAA comparison still cannot be completed: cost for the Snowbird and Killington shows is UNKNOWN, and the 2026 event recap carries no cost data for any of the eight events.
